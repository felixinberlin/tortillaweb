Chronicle of the Spanish Potato Omelette: Evolution, Identity and Contemporary Reality
1. Introduction: The Icon of Spanish Gastronomic Identity

The Spanish potato omelette (tortilla de patatas) is not merely a food product in the Iberian Peninsula; it represents a fundamental pillar of culinary identity and a symbol of social cohesion. Its presence is omnipresent, from the most intimate domestic environments to the showcases of collective catering and the emerging delivery sector. This dish transcends mere nutrition to become a shared sensory heritage for millions of people.

According to the analysis of contemporary consumption habits, the cultural values that support the dominance of this dish are:

High Identity Value: It is perceived as the ultimate reference point of Spanish gastronomy.
Broad Popular Acceptance: Its consumption is widespread across all socioeconomic groups.
Versatility and Resilience: It has demonstrated a remarkable ability to adapt from the traditional homemade format to industrial fourth- and fifth-range food solutions.
Emotional Connection: It is associated with collective memory and gastronomic “comfort”, maintaining its status as a traditional dish even within mass-production contexts.
2. Critical Milestones in History and Sociological Evolution

The persistence of the potato omelette in the Spanish collective imagination can be explained through a historical framework that has consolidated its status first as a food of survival and later as a source of pleasure. The evolution of the dish reflects the history of modern Spain itself.

Timeline of Gastronomic Identity
1798 (Extremadura): First documented references in the town of Villanueva de la Serena, where it emerged as an ingenious and nutritious response to a context of scarcity.
1817 (Navarre): Documentation of the recipe in rural Navarre, becoming established as an affordable protein solution in times of food shortages.
The General Zumalacárregui Myth: A legendary account places the invention of the dish during the Carlist Wars, describing it as a logistical solution to feed troops quickly and efficiently.
21st Century (Contemporary Era): The dish has evolved from its subsistence roots into a phenomenon of “gourmetization”, becoming a central element of the ready-to-eat market and the subject of national debates about the ideal degree of cooking.
3. The Sociological Phenomenon of the “Runny” Tortilla (Poco Cuajada)

Today, we face a cultural paradox that can be described as a “hygienic-sanitary challenge in defence of sensory heritage”. There is a clear tension between food safety guidelines and popular taste, where the pursuit of a juicy texture dominates the perception of risk.

According to CIS (2024) statistical data, preferences regarding the cooking level in Spain reveal a clear dominance of juicy tortillas:

Cooking level	Preference
Undercooked / lightly set (poco hecha)	53.9%
Fully cooked (muy hecha)	28.8%
Medium / ideal point (en su punto)	17.3%

Analysis: It is fascinating to observe how the option representing the greatest microbiological risk is precisely the choice of more than half of the population. This phenomenon of “culinary risk” demonstrates that Spanish consumers prioritise the sensory experience and the traditional “juicy centre” above health warnings, revealing cultural resistance against the homogenisation of food safety standards.

4. Consumption and Habits: The Tortilla in the Modern Shopping Basket

The analysis of contemporary food culture reveals a deep sociological tension: while 59.1% of Spaniards state that they cook daily at home, this habit coexists with explosive growth in convenience products.

137 eggs per person/year: This is the average egg consumption in Spain, with an estimated 20% specifically used for preparing tortillas.
6.6% increase in prepared dishes: Sustained growth during the last financial year (2024), reflecting lack of time in urban lifestyles.
48% growth in “ready-to-eat” products: Purchases of pre-made tortillas in supermarkets have surged over the last two years, becoming the leading product in this category.
Habit Contrast: Despite industrialisation, the tortilla remains at the centre of batch cooking (practised by 36% of the population) and is the main element of the weekly packed lunch (táper).
5. Challenges of Tradition in the 21st Century: Safety and Historical Outbreaks

The recent history of this gastronomic icon has been marked by salmonellosis outbreaks that have reshaped handling practices. The pathogen Salmonella Enteritidis presents particular danger due to its ability to contaminate eggs internally, meaning that practices such as washing the outside of the shell are ineffective in guaranteeing safety in lightly cooked preparations.

Timeline of Significant Alerts and Outbreaks (1991–2025)
Year	Location	Context and Impact
1991	Valencia	Historic food-service outbreak with fatal outcome.
2021	Soria	Severe impact among vulnerable groups (paediatric and adult ICU cases).
2023	Casa Dani (Madrid)	Large outbreak with 101 affected people and 13 hospitalisations in a renowned establishment.
2024	Barcelona	Restaurant incident affecting around 30 people.
2025 (Feb)	Zaragoza (Aragon)	Health alert in restaurant with at least 4 confirmed cases.
2025 (Jun)	Trasan Fest (Galicia)	Critical incident with more than 150 affected people and a peak of 41 hospitalisations.
Critical Factors and the “Danger Zone”

Epidemiological analyses, especially from the summer outbreaks in Barcelona and Galicia, identify that Spanish summer temperatures (above 35°C) act as critical catalysts. Keeping tortillas within the danger zone (7–48°C) allows a minimal bacterial load, protected inside the “barely set” centre, to multiply exponentially within only a few hours.

6. Conclusion: The Future of a Culinary Legacy

The future of the Spanish potato omelette lies in harmonising its juicy essence with scientifically based safety protocols. To preserve this legacy without compromising public health, the following evidence-based technical guidelines are proposed:

Professionalisation of Texture: In the restaurant sector, the use of pasteurised egg products should become the absolute standard for all tortillas that do not reach complete coagulation, ensuring safety without sacrificing juiciness.
Critical Temperature Management: Consumers must be made aware of the vulnerability of fresh eggs. To ensure safety, an internal temperature of 70°C should be reached for at least 2 minutes or, as an intermediate safety threshold, 63°C for 20 seconds.
Control of Environmental Exposure: Especially in warm climates and mass events, tortillas should be kept under strict refrigeration or hot holding (≥63°C), eliminating the habit of leaving the dish at room temperature, which can transform a culinary treasure into an avoidable biological risk.
